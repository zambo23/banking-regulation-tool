# Banking Regulators: BIS, EU, EBA, ECB, DNB

Global standards from the BIS are turned into EU law, detailed by the EBA, and applied to banks by the ECB and DNB. This doc covers each body's goals, responsibilities, legal power, operation, binding power and role in banking regulation.

Note: EUR-Lex is not a regulator. It is the EU's official online database of law, run by the Publications Office of the EU. The institutions that make EU banking law are the European Commission, the European Parliament and the Council; "EUR-Lex" below covers that legislative layer.

## 1. BIS – Bank for International Settlements (Basel)

The BIS sets global banking standards through the Basel Committee, but they bind no one until a jurisdiction writes them into law.

**Goals:** Support central banks in pursuing monetary and financial stability through international cooperation; often called the "central bank for central banks."

**Responsibilities**

- Hosts the **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS)**, which writes the global prudential standards for banks: Basel I, II and III, and the Core Principles for Effective Banking Supervision.
- Hosts other standard-setting bodies, such as the CPMI (payments) and IAIS (insurance), and works closely with the Financial Stability Board.
- Does research and economic analysis, and provides banking services (reserve management) to central banks.

**Legal power:** An international organisation founded by treaty in 1930, owned by about 60 central banks. No legal authority over banks or countries.

**How it operates:** Consensus among member central banks and supervisors. The BCBS drafts standards, consults publicly, publishes final frameworks and monitors implementation through peer reviews (RCAP assessments).

**Binding power:** None directly. Basel standards are "soft law" and become binding only when a jurisdiction writes them into its own law (in the EU, the CRR/CRD). Peer pressure and market expectations make compliance strong in practice.

## 2. EU legislators (law published on EUR-Lex)

The EU legislators turn Basel standards into binding law that applies across all member states.

**Goals:** A single rulebook for banking across the EU, protecting the internal market and financial stability.

**Responsibilities**

- The **Commission** proposes legislation and adopts delegated and implementing acts, often turning EBA drafts into binding technical standards.
- The **Parliament and Council** negotiate and adopt legislation as co-legislators.
- Key banking texts:
  - **CRR**, Regulation 575/2013 (directly applicable)
  - **CRD**, Directive 2013/36/EU (transposed into national law)
  - **BRRD**, the bank recovery and resolution directive
  - **SSM Regulation** (1024/2013) and **SRM Regulation** (806/2014)
  - **DGSD**, the deposit guarantee schemes directive
- The latest Basel III package (CRR3/CRD6) mostly applies from 1 January 2025; some parts, such as the market-risk rules (FRTB), were postponed.

**Legal power:** Derived from the EU Treaties (TFEU, e.g. Art. 114 on the internal market).

**Binding power:** High.

- **Regulations** apply directly in all member states.
- **Directives** bind member states as to the result; each state transposes them. In the Netherlands, mainly into the Wft (Financial Supervision Act).
- The **Court of Justice of the EU** interprets the law.

## 3. EBA – European Banking Authority (Paris)

The EBA writes the detailed technical rules and guidelines, but does not supervise individual banks.

**Goals:** A single rulebook and consistent supervisory practice across all EU countries, including those outside the euro area.

**Responsibilities**

- Drafts **Regulatory and Implementing Technical Standards (RTS/ITS)**, which the Commission adopts as binding acts.
- Issues **guidelines and recommendations**.
- Runs the EU-wide **stress tests**, together with the ECB and the ESRB.
- Sets supervisory reporting frameworks (COREP/FINREP) and answers interpretation questions through its Q&A tool.
- Does peer reviews, mediates disputes between national authorities, and protects consumers.
- Some anti-money-laundering tasks are moving to the new AMLA.

**Legal power:** Established by Regulation 1093/2010; part of the European System of Financial Supervision alongside ESMA, EIOPA and the ESRB.

**How it operates:** Its Board of Supervisors is made up of the heads of the national supervisors; the ECB attends as a non-voting member. Work runs through standing committees, public consultations and impact assessments.

**Binding power**

- Technical standards become legally binding once the Commission adopts them.
- Guidelines work on **"comply or explain"**: authorities must comply or publicly explain why not.
- Limited direct powers in emergencies and cases of breach of EU law.

## 4. ECB – European Central Bank (Frankfurt)

The ECB directly supervises the euro area's significant banks through the Single Supervisory Mechanism, and its decisions bind them.

**Goals:** Price stability in the euro area (primary mandate). Since 2014, also safe and sound banks and an integrated banking system.

**Responsibilities**

- **Monetary policy** for the euro area.
- **Banking supervision through the Single Supervisory Mechanism (SSM):**
  - Directly supervises **significant institutions**, roughly 110 banking groups, including ING, Rabobank and ABN AMRO.
  - Oversees how national authorities supervise smaller banks.
  - Grants and withdraws **banking licences** for all euro-area banks.
  - Approves qualifying holdings (acquisitions of significant stakes in banks).
- Runs the annual **SREP**, which sets bank-specific Pillar 2 capital requirements, and conducts on-site inspections.
- Assesses fit and proper requirements for bank management.
- Can impose sanctions.

**Legal power:** TFEU Art. 127(6) and the SSM Regulation. It applies EU law and national transpositions of directives, including the Dutch Wft.

**How it operates**

- The **Supervisory Board** (ECB members and national supervisors) prepares decisions.
- The **Governing Council** adopts them under a "non-objection" procedure.
- Day-to-day supervision is done by **Joint Supervisory Teams** mixing ECB and national staff.
- Monetary policy and supervision are organisationally separated.

**Binding power:** High and direct. Decisions bind the banks they are addressed to. The ECB can issue its own regulations; its guides and expectations are not legally binding but are strongly followed. Decisions can be challenged before the CJEU.

## 5. DNB – De Nederlandsche Bank (Amsterdam)

DNB is the Dutch supervisor within the SSM: it directly supervises less significant banks and works inside the ECB's teams for the large ones.

**Goals:** Financial stability in the Netherlands, and a secure, reliable financial system.

**Responsibilities**

- **National Competent Authority within the SSM:**
  - Takes part in the Joint Supervisory Teams for significant Dutch banks.
  - Directly supervises **less significant banks**, under ECB oversight.
- Prudential supervisor of pension funds, insurers, payment and e-money institutions, and trust offices.
- Integrity supervision, including anti-money-laundering rules under the Wwft.
- **National Resolution Authority** within the Single Resolution Mechanism.
- Runs the **Deposit Guarantee Scheme**, protecting deposits up to €100,000.
- **Macroprudential authority**, including setting the countercyclical capital buffer and the systemic-risk buffer.
- As part of the Eurosystem, implements ECB monetary policy and oversees payment systems.

**Legal power:** Mainly the Bank Act 1998 (Bankwet) and the Wft, which transposes EU directives, plus the SSM Regulation.

**How it operates:** A twin-peaks model with the **AFM**: DNB handles prudential supervision, the AFM conduct of business. It works with the Ministry of Finance on legislation.

**Binding power:** Binding decisions, fines, penalty payments and instructions to the institutions it supervises. For significant banks, final prudential decisions rest with the ECB.

## How they work together

Each layer turns the one above into something more concrete and more binding, ending with the supervisors who apply it to banks.

```text
BIS / BCBS ──► EU legislators (CRR, CRD) ──► EBA (RTS/ITS, guidelines) ──► ECB (significant banks) / DNB (less significant banks) ──► banks
```

Supervisory experience flows back up: the ECB and DNB sit in the BCBS, and national supervisors and the ECB sit in the EBA, so practice feeds into new standards.

**Related bodies:** the **SRB** (Single Resolution Board) for resolving failing banks, the **ESRB** for systemic risk, the **AFM** for conduct supervision in the Netherlands, and **AMLA** for anti-money-laundering supervision.

## Summary table

| Institution | Type | Sets rules? | Supervises banks? | Binding on banks? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| BIS / BCBS | International organisation | Global standards | No | No (soft law) |
| EU legislators (EUR-Lex) | EU institutions / database | Yes, primary law | No | Yes (regulations directly; directives via national law) |
| EBA | EU agency | Technical standards, guidelines | No (coordinates) | Standards yes; guidelines comply or explain |
| ECB | EU institution / central bank | Supervisory regulations and guides | Yes, significant banks | Yes, decisions |
